SkillSim Lite

SkillSim lets people rehearse hard workplace conversations with an AI character who has their own motives and pushes back. It first decodes the situation's unwritten expectations, then gives feedback grounded in exactly what the person said.

Section 2

2Motivation

Who has this problem

Some workplace conversations carry real stakes and follow rules nobody writes down: telling a direct report their work is slipping, countering a job offer, calming a client after your team made a mistake. People who grew up around professional offices pick these rules up informally. Many others don't, including:

  • International students and early-career immigrants. U.S. colleges hosted 1,177,766 international students in 2024/25, and 294,253 were on Optional Practical Training, working in U.S. jobs, often for the first time [2].
  • First-generation professionals. One workforce program starts from the observation that first-generation graduates "might not be aware of certain 'unwritten rules'" at work [3].
  • New managers and anyone facing a first-of-its-kind conversation, such as a first salary negotiation or a first escalation from an unhappy client.

Why it's hard if you're new to professional norms

The difficulty usually isn't vocabulary. It's interpretation and timing. "Let me see what I can do" may signal real flexibility or a polite close. "It's been a lot" can be an invitation to ask more. A manager's mention of the "performance review" can land as a threat even when it isn't meant as one. Power dynamics shape what the other person can say aloud: a direct report may not admit that a VP pulled them onto other work, and a recruiter won't reveal the top of a salary band. These meanings vary by workplace and culture, so there's rarely a single correct script.

Current workarounds

  • Reading generic advice (articles, books, career-center handouts): accessible but not interactive, and silent about what you actually said.
  • Practicing with a mentor or friend: effective but scarce, socially costly to repeat, and dependent on who you happen to know.
  • Paid coaching or corporate training platforms: often strong, but usually bought by employers for specific roles such as sales (see Section 3).
  • Asking a general chatbot to role-play: instantly available, but the user has to supply the scenario, the other person's motives, and the evaluation criteria themselves.

Why it matters

Employers say they want these skills. In NACE's Job Outlook 2026 (Spring Update), as reported by Iowa State University, 78% of employers seek verbal communication skills and 72% written communication skills on a candidate's résumé [1]. A mishandled conversation has concrete costs: a negotiated salary that stays lower, a direct report who disengages, a client account at risk. The people least likely to have a mentor to rehearse with are often the ones navigating the least familiar norms. SkillSim's premise is that a low-cost, private, repeatable rehearsal could help close part of that gap. Hypothesis That premise has not yet been tested with users (Section 7).

Section 3

3What is new, and what isn't

AI role-play for workplace conversations already exists, and several products do it well. Each product page below was reviewed on October 24, 2026. Capabilities are summarized from each vendor's own public description, which we did not independently test.

ProductPrimary focusWhat it offers (vendor's description)
Yoodli [4]AI role-play coaching for sales and enablement teams, also managers and leadersRole-play personas and scenarios with rubrics. Feedback on delivery (pacing, tone) and content. Lists use cases including "manager coaching" and "difficult conversations". Enterprise customization and integrations.
Second Nature [5]Sales and support team trainingConversational AI "virtual pitch partner" with avatars and multi-persona meetings. Scoring, real-time feedback, manager analytics, custom scenarios, 30+ languages.
Huru [6]Job-interview practice for individualsLive AI interviewer that adapts follow-up questions. Scoring with per-answer criteria, delivery analysis, and "moments to rework", each with an exact quote and a stronger alternative. Free for individuals.
ChatGPT [7]General-purpose assistantWill role-play when asked, including by voice. Custom GPTs can carry instructions and files. The user designs the scenario and decides what good feedback looks like.

Two things SkillSim does are therefore not new. Practicing difficult conversations with an AI partner is offered by Yoodli and Second Nature. Feedback that quotes the user and proposes a better line is offered by Huru.

What SkillSim does differently

SkillSim's focus is narrower: helping someone who doesn't know the unwritten expectations of a situation, rather than training a sales team or rehearsing an interview. Within that focus, the design choices are these:

  • Decode this situation, before practicing. A separate agent explains likely meanings of workplace phrases ("band", "internal equity", "blocked", "in writing"), the power dynamics, what the user can't know yet, and questions they could ask. It is grounded in a small curated knowledge base, and the app labels it as "not universal, not externally verified." We did not find this step described on the product pages above. That is not proof that no product offers it.
  • A character with private motivations and proportionate resistance. Each scenario has hidden motives (for example, Jordan was pulled onto a VP's project and is embarrassed). The character reveals them only when the user's approach would realistically earn it, and looks up facts with a tool instead of inventing them.
  • Feedback that is checked, not just generated. The server rejects any "quote" that doesn't appear verbatim in the user's own messages, drops praise without a quote, removes comments on personal traits (personality, accent, gender, and similar), frames interpretations as possibilities, and presents its alternative as one valid style among several. Huru also quotes users. The difference is the server-side verification and the explicit rules about framing.
  • Transparent cost and safety. Every request is metered and inspectable in a developer trace, and the defenses are tested (Sections 5 and 6).

What the others have that SkillSim doesn't: voice and video, delivery metrics (pace, filler words), enterprise administration, many more scenarios, and real user bases. SkillSim is a three-scenario, text-only prototype.

Section 4

4The agents

SkillSim uses three AI agents with separate jobs, separate information, and separate permissions. The FastAPI server orchestrates them: no agent calls another directly. When the user clicks End, the server passes the full transcript from the conversation to the FeedbackAgent. When the user retries, the server copies the previous feedback's retry challenge into the new session and shows it to the user; it is not shown to the character.

AgentJob and what it seesTool (read-only)Output and checks
DecodeAgentExplains phrases, dynamics, unknowns, and questions before practice. Sees only what the user can see: no private motivations or facts.lookup_workplace_knowledge(section): 5 sections of knowledge_base.jsonStrict JSON schema (DecodeOut), validated with Pydantic. Phrases not in the curated notes are removed. Cached once per scenario.
RoleplayAgentPlays the character, one reply per user turn. Sees the character's profile, private motivations, resistance rules, boundaries, and the conversation; never the user's objective or the scoring criteria.get_scenario_facts(topic): that scenario's curated factsPlain dialogue, at most 400 output tokens. The output guard rejects replies that leave character, repeat hidden instructions, contain secrets, claim legal or official authority, or state figures found nowhere in the scenario or the user's words. It regenerates once, then blocks.
FeedbackAgentReviews the finished conversation as a separate reviewer. Sees the user's role, objective, criteria, and the transcript (marked as data, not instructions).get_workplace_guidance(topic): curated guidance.json (it may call this; it isn't required to)Strict JSON schema (FeedbackOut) with six parts plus a skill check, validated with Pydantic (one retry). Quotes are verified, trait comments removed, scores clamped to 1–5, and the overall score recomputed by the server.

Model. All agents use gpt-6-luna through the course's Portkey gateway (Responses API), the only model the course key allows. It is inexpensive ($0.10 input / $0.50 output per million tokens; see Section 5) and fast enough for conversation. Reasoning effort is set to none because each step is short and concrete. Instructions, scenario data, and user text always travel in separate message channels.

One real run, step by step

The following trace is session ef22e1abf81b ("Delivering tough feedback"), run in the browser on October 24, 2026 with the user's lines typed fresh. All 8 model requests were new generations (Portkey cache status MISS). The app labels it "Attempt 2". An earlier attempt with the benchmark's exact wording was answered entirely from the gateway's response cache (Section 5), so it was set aside and is not shown.

Step 1: Select and decode. The user reads the briefing, then opens Decode this situation. The DecodeAgent's output was generated once for this scenario (with real tool calls to the knowledge base) and is served from SkillSim's cache. That cached generation is the decode shown here.

Step 2: Conversation (RoleplayAgent). Jordan is vague at first ("it's been a lot"). After the user's empathetic second question, Jordan reveals the hidden reason: rebrand work that Dana Ortiz (the VP of Marketing, per the scenario facts) asked for, about 12 hours a week, never in the sprint plan. Before stating those details, the agent made 3 real tool calls: get_scenario_facts(missed_deadlines), get_scenario_facts(workload), get_scenario_facts(next_deadline). The full transcript:

Jordan (agent)Hey! I've got a few minutes before my next review. What did you want to cover today?
Manager (user)Hi Jordan, thanks for making time. I want to talk about the last three sprints: the checkout mockups arrived late each time, and the engineers are now stuck waiting on saved carts. What's been happening on your end?
Jordan (agent)Yeah, it’s been a lot. I know the mockups have been late, and I’m not happy about engineers being stuck, but I’ve been trying to keep too many things moving at once.
Manager (user)That sounds like a lot to juggle, and I'm not here to blame you. What are the things you've been trying to keep moving?
Jordan (agent)I’ve been doing the checkout work, and there’s also been the marketing rebrand. Dana Ortiz asked me to help with it, and it’s been taking around 12 hours a week. It wasn’t in the sprint plan, but I didn’t feel great saying no to her.
Manager (user)Thanks for telling me. Twelve hours a week is real work, and it should have been visible in our plan. I can raise it with Dana. If the rebrand pauses, what do you think is realistic for the saved-carts handoff?
Jordan (agent)If the rebrand pauses, I can focus on saved carts and get the final mockups to engineering by Thursday. I’d want to keep the scope tight and do a quick check-in if anything starts slipping.
Manager (user)Good. Then here's the plan: I'll talk to Dana by tomorrow afternoon, you'll deliver the saved-carts mockups by Thursday, and we'll do a quick check-in Wednesday. Does that work for you?
Jordan (agent)Yes, that works. I’ll keep the scope focused on saved carts and bring any blockers to our Wednesday check-in instead of trying to absorb them quietly.

Step 3: Feedback (FeedbackAgent). After End, the server sent the transcript to the FeedbackAgent. Its JSON passed schema validation, and every quote shown was verified against the user's messages. Overall skill check: 90/100. Retry challenge: "After Jordan explains an obstacle, ask what support or priority change would help, then confirm a specific deliverable, owner, and date together."

Section 5

5Tokenomics

Every model request is logged with the usage the API returned: input, cached input, and output tokens, plus latency, tool calls, errors, and Portkey's gateway cache status. Prices come from one file, pricing.json: $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens, from the course starter code (dated 2026-10-06). No cached-input rate is published, so cached tokens are billed at the full input rate. Every cost below is therefore an upper bound.

A measurement trap we found. The course gateway caches whole responses. Identical requests come back in a fraction of a second carrying the original token counts. Our first benchmark repeated identical scripts, and its later runs were mostly replays. We now log the cache status of every request, force fresh generations in benchmarks, and average only sessions in which every request was a fresh model call.

Cost optimizations and their measured effect

1. Compact context (adopted). The same content is sent as compact JSON instead of indented JSON, repeat fact lookups are not re-sent, and a redundant topic list is removed. With identical inputs, the first request of every session shrank by 93–96 tokens (7.4–8.1%), identically on all three repetitions. Across whole sessions, though, the difference (-1.9% input tokens, -3.3% cost) is smaller than the run-to-run variation, so no session-level saving is claimed.

2. Prefetching facts (tested, not adopted). Putting the character's facts in the cacheable prefix cut lookup requests per session from 5.0 to 3.9 and total requests by -13.0%, but input tokens changed by only -4.7% and cost by -3.2%, again within noise. It also gives the character every fact from the first turn, which risks earlier reveals.

Test results

Offline suite: 78 tests, result OK (run while building this report). The 28 security tests include a malicious prompt injection, an API-key extraction attempt, session and daily budget exhaustion, invalid structured output (fails safely; recovers on retry), and a normal successful run. What testing found: the first live budget test exposed a real defect. The budget was checked before each model call but not before each turn, so a turn could spend tokens and then fail. A check before each turn now refuses such turns with zero model calls. Separately, a check in our own evidence script had been written so it could never fail; it was corrected before the results below were produced.

Section 7

7Impact

No real users have tested SkillSim yet. The participant data file holds 0 consented rows, so there are no usability ratings, completion times, testimonials, or learning outcomes to report. The study protocol (10–15 minutes per person, separate consent for data, quotes, and transcripts) and an analysis script are ready in evaluation/. Everything below is internal, automated evidence, which is weaker.

  • Actionability: both B and C gave a concrete alternative line. The "next step" proxy undervalues B, whose "what to improve" list was plausibly actionable.

Feedback calibration check Internal, scripted

To see whether scores track quality, the author scripted a deliberately weak attempt at the upset-client scenario (technical jargon and blaming the client's test data; session 6d77719942ca) and a strong one (ownership, a plan, dates; 15f0757aac0d). They scored 30 and 80. This shows the scores move in the expected direction for an obvious contrast. It does not show that real users improve.

What the potential impact would look like Hypothesis

If the usability study confirms that people find the practice useful, the measured cost makes broad access plausible. At the mean measured cost, eight practice sessions per user per month comes to about $0.0171 per user, and serving 10,000 users would cost roughly $170.56 per month in model fees (Section 5). A university career center or an ESL program could plausibly offer it to everyone, not only to people who can find a mentor. That depends on outcomes we haven't measured: whether users find the decode panel accurate, whether practice transfers to real conversations, and whether the feedback feels fair to people from different backgrounds.

Section 8

8Limitations and next steps

Technical limitations

  • Local prototype. Not deployed; sessions are stored as unencrypted JSON on one machine; per-browser IDs are not accounts.
  • Latency and cost. About 2.7 s of model time per user turn in the traced run, and about four times the cost of a plain chatbot, mostly from fact-lookup requests (half of all input tokens).
  • Guard limits. The figure check can't catch a character agreeing to a number the user named. Regex-based checks can produce false positives or miss paraphrased leaks.
  • Text only. There is no voice or delivery feedback, which the established products in Section 3 do offer.

Uncertainty about feedback quality

The feedback is validated for form (schema, real quotes, no trait judgments), not for truth. Nobody has yet checked whether its interpretations are accurate or whether its scores agree with an experienced manager's judgment. Our quality rubric hit its ceiling on every run, so it couldn't detect small regressions. One model reviews another model's role-play, and both may share blind spots. The curated knowledge base reflects US-centric office norms written by one author.

Limited scenarios

Three scenarios (management feedback, salary negotiation, client recovery), each with fixed facts and one counterpart. Real situations involve more people, more history, and cultural context the scenarios don't capture.

Product-market fit hypotheses Untested

  • H1: International students on OPT and first-generation graduates will value Decode this situation more than general-purpose chatbot practice.
  • H2: University career centers and English-language programs, not individuals, are the most likely buyers, because they already pay for interview-practice tools.
  • H3: Users will retry a scenario when given a single, specific challenge rather than a long list of suggestions.

Next month, in order

  1. Run the usability protocol with at least five participants from the target groups, and have a career advisor rate the A/B/C outputs blind.
  2. Calibrate feedback against human raters, and add a rubric that isn't capped by the ceiling problem.
  3. Let career-center staff author scenarios through a form, with review, so coverage grows beyond three.
  4. Deploy with accounts, encrypted storage, and retention controls; then consider voice.

The longer-term vision

A library of the conversations nobody teaches, written with people who have had them, and decoded honestly: here is what this phrase usually means, here is what you can't know, here is a question you could ask. Then a safe place to practice them until they feel familiar.
